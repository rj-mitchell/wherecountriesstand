Methodology

How Where Countries Stand reads a question, finds the statements and votes in the UN’s records that bear on it, writes a briefing with AI and checks the briefing against those records.

AI briefings

Briefings are written by AI. Each one is labelled as written by AI, and each is checked against the records it cites before it is shown.

What the model is given

The AI that writes a briefing is given only the UN records found for your question, and may use nothing else, even for well-known facts, dates or names. Those records are statements made in the Security Council, in the General Assembly’s General Debate and in its other plenary meetings, the General Assembly’s recorded votes and the Security Council’s votes (The record).

The newest General Debate speeches come from the UN’s website first and are labelled as not yet the official record. The General Debate’s verbatim records are published about a year after each debate. Until then, a speech comes from the UN’s own General Debate website: the text the delegation issued, as prepared, or an AI transcript of the UN’s English interpretation. The label sits beside the source, and the record replaces the text when it is published.

Evidence is drawn from across groups of countries: by region as a rule, by bloc where a question compares blocs, or by place at the Security Council (permanent, elected and invited members). The best statement is taken from each group in turn, with no more than two from any one country unless the question asks what particular countries said. A country the question names is always included if it has said something relevant on the subject, and the summary above the sources says when it has not. Briefings report in neutral words (Impartiality).

The UN’s records are read in twice a day: new Security Council meetings, General Assembly votes and General Debate records. The home page shows the latest date held for each, and the status page shows when the record was last read.

How it is checked

Each point links to its source, and each source to where it was published. Once a briefing is written, every citation is checked against the source text, quotations are checked word for word, and sentences that no source supports are flagged (From question to briefing gives the detail). The checks catch many errors but not all.

If the record holds nothing relevant, the site says “No records found”. When the search finds nothing at all, no briefing is written. When it finds only passing mentions, the AI is told to say “No records found” and what the records do cover.

The site holds Security Council verbatim records from 1992, the General Assembly’s General Debate and recorded votes from 1946, its other plenary meetings from 1994, and the Security Council’s votes from 1946, all searchable and linked. Security Council statements, and General Debate statements taken from the UN’s verbatim records, carry their document symbols (S/PV, A/[session]/PV); votes carry their resolution numbers. Unless a question gives dates, a briefing draws on the last ten years.

The method and the code are published, every briefing is labelled as written by AI, and the site is free and doesn’t track you. The method is on this page, the code is on GitHub under the licences set out on the About page, and the privacy notice sets out the little that is stored.

The record

The Security Council’s debates are held from 1992, from the UN Security Council Debates corpus (Schoenfeld and others, CC0) to the end of 2023, and from the UN’s verbatim records (S/PV) since, checked for new meetings twice a day.

The General Assembly’s General Debate is held in full: every country’s annual statement, from the UN General Debate Corpus (Jankin, Baturo and Dasandi, CC0) and, for recent sessions, the General Assembly’s verbatim records (A/[session]/PV). Until a session’s record is published, about a year after the debate, statements come from the UN’s General Debate website, and are marked as not yet the official record. Rights of reply are included as records of their own.

The General Assembly’s other plenary meetings are held from its 49th session (1994), with its emergency special and special sessions, from the UN’s verbatim records (A/[session]/PV): every statement but the President’s, labelled with its session, meeting and agenda item. Records before 1994 are printed in a way the site cannot yet read reliably. Statements made in explanation of a vote are linked to the vote they explain where the record makes that certain: the statement names the resolution or its draft, or the vote was the only one taken in its round. Each vote’s page lists them.

Every recorded vote in the General Assembly since 1946 comes from the UN Digital Library: the voting data of the UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library, with each Member State’s vote on each resolution adopted by recorded vote. It leaves out votes on separate paragraphs and drafts that were not adopted. Each vote has a page of its own, written from that data with no AI, with how each Member State voted, the totals, and links to the resolution, the meeting record and the UN Digital Library’s record: see Recorded votes in the General Assembly.

Every resolution the Security Council has adopted since 1946 comes from the Security Council voting data of the UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library, with each member’s vote. The drafts it did not adopt are not in that data: they are read from the Council’s meeting records the site holds, from 1992, with how each member voted and the President’s announcement of the result in the record’s own words. A draft is not adopted when it does not get nine votes in favour, or when a permanent member votes against it (commonly called a veto). Each veto in the Library’s list of vetoes since 1992 is checked against the records; vetoes before 1992 are given as that list gives them, with the permanent members voting against and no other member’s vote. Each vote has a page of its own, written with no AI: see Votes in the Security Council.

Each Member State, observer State and former Member State, and Kosovo, which speaks in the Security Council’s record, has a page of its own, written from the same records with no AI: its groups on the day, its latest statements, its recorded votes and its votes in the Security Council, counted and listed but never compared with another country’s. Which places have a page follows the UN’s own lists and records: see Countries in the record.

From question to briefing

The question is read for its subject, dates, countries and groups, shown as filters above each briefing. The record is searched by keyword and by meaning; statements by heads of State or Government and ministers carry a little more weight, and recent statements more than old ones. Countries are grouped by their memberships on the date. An AI model then writes the briefing from those sources only, and every sentence is checked against them. The results are shown at the top of each briefing, checked against the UN record: how many citations matched their sources, how many quotations were found word for word, and how many sentences have no source.

In the briefing, each point is followed by its source, named by country or group, body and date; pressing it shows the passage from the record with the quoted words highlighted. A quotation found word for word is set in the record’s typeface on a highlight, with “word for word” and a tick. A sentence that no source supports is marked with a dotted underline, and the first with “No source”; so is a sentence whose citations all failed the check (the citations are removed). A quotation not found word for word has a wavy underline and says so, and judgemental words in the model’s own voice are underlined. A quotation found word for word in the source cited straight after it is shown as that source’s, even where the model attached the citation to the words beside it, such as a date. Every mark is also given in words to screen readers.

Impartiality

Briefings report the positions countries took and add no view of their own. The site’s own voice does not use labels such as “regime” or “rogue state”. Statements made on behalf of a group are attributed to the group. Countries are named as the UN names them.

Something wrong or unfair in a briefing can be reported on the corrections page. Delegations, governments and people named in the record can also read If you’re named on this site.
