If you’re named

If you speak for a country at the United Nations, or you are named in the UN’s records, and something this site says about you is wrong or unfair, or you would like to say something in your own words, this is what you can do.

Who this is for

  • A country’s delegation or permanent mission to the United Nations, and its government: what the site says a country said, how it voted, and the groups it is counted in.
  • People named in the UN’s records: heads of State and Government, ministers, representatives and UN officials, as the record names them when they speak.
  • Someone acting for them, such as a mission’s press office or a foreign ministry.

Everyone the record names spoke in a public role. If something about you that should not be shown appears on the site all the same, ask for it to be removed: these requests are dealt with first.

What it covers

Everything the site says about a country or a person, not only briefings:

  • groups of countries, counted by membership on the date: the regions, the UN’s regional groups, the permanent and elected members of the Security Council and the non-members invited to speak, and the blocs and lists (the African Union, the European Union, the Group of 77, the Non-Aligned Movement, NATO, the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, least developed countries, landlocked developing countries and small island developing States), the World Bank’s income groups, and suspensions from the African Union’s activities, as the glossary describes them;
  • how briefings group and balance countries by those groups, and the counts of statements, countries and meetings shown above a briefing;
  • country names and status (Member State, observer State), as the UN gives them on the date;
  • speaker labels: the country, organisation or UN office a statement is attributed to, the speaker’s role, the President of the Security Council speaking for the Council or for their own country, statements made on behalf of a group and attributed to it, and “(spoke in French)” with its note that the English text is the UN’s translation;
  • the labels saying a text is not yet the official record (a text from the UN’s General Debate website, issued by the delegation, or transcribed from the UN’s interpretation), or that it was recognised from a scanned record;
  • rights of reply in the General Debate, which the site keeps apart from the statements they answer;
  • connections the site draws, such as the link from a country named in a question to its page on Where Lives Stand;
  • briefings, and what they say the record shows.

Mistakes in the UN’s records themselves can only be corrected by the United Nations, through its own procedure for corrections to verbatim records.

Three routes

  1. Something is wrong

    A fact, a quotation, a date, a label, a group or a connection is incorrect, or a briefing misstates the record. We correct it or withdraw it as soon as we can, and say publicly what changed and when, on the corrections page.

    Tell us what’s wrong

  2. You’ve been characterised unfairly

    The record is right, but the site’s own words, labels or groupings are unfair: a loaded word in the site’s own voice, a connection without published evidence, or a grouping that suggests something the record doesn’t show. We check it against Where They Stand’s impartiality policy and, if it breaks the policy, put it right in the same way.

    Tell us what’s unfair

  3. Add a statement in your own words

    The record is accurate and the site has followed the policy, but you see it differently. You can add a short statement, shown beside the briefing it is about.

    Add a statement

Statements in your own words

  • What it can say: up to 150 words, in your own name, about the briefing it sits beside. Point to the record where you can (a UN document, another statement in the record, a published statement by your mission or government), with a link.
  • What it can’t contain: anything defamatory or abusive; anything about a private individual, or personal information about anyone else; appeals for votes, money or support; or comments on another person’s conduct. During election periods, statements are still accepted, but not ones that campaign.
  • How it is shown: beside the briefing, headed “Statement from [your name], [date]” and set apart from the site’s own text, as below. The site has no page for each country yet; when it has, a country’s statements will be shown on its page too. A statement is published as you wrote it, or declined with our reasons. It is never edited, except to take out something these rules don’t allow, with your agreement.
  • Never part of the record: it is never given to the AI model that writes briefings, never searched when a briefing is prepared, and never treated as part of the UN’s record.
  • Changing it: you can update or withdraw it at any time, by emailing admin@wherecountriesstand.com from the address you gave. We can remove a statement that breaks these rules, and we will tell you why.

How we check who you are

Before a statement is published, or changed or withdrawn at your request, we confirm that it comes from you or someone acting for you. We write to the email address you give: an address at your permanent mission or your foreign ministry is quickest. If you use another, we may ask you to confirm from an address your mission or government publishes. Reports that something is wrong or unfair don’t need this.

The address is used only for this. It is kept while your statement is waiting or shown, and deleted three months after it is withdrawn or declined (see the privacy notice).

How long it takes

We reply within 5 working days, and decide within 20 working days.

Anything that may be unlawful or harmful, and requests to remove something about a person, are dealt with sooner. If a concern looks well founded but needs time to decide, we may hide the briefing meanwhile, with a note.

Who decides, and appeals

Where They Stand’s founder decides, until its trustees appoint someone else. Every concern, the decision and its reasons are logged, and the trustees review the log at each meeting.

If you are unhappy with a decision, you can ask the trustees to review it: email admin@wheretheystand.uk. If you are still unhappy, you can raise concerns about the charity with the Charity Commission.

Letters claiming legal liability, such as defamation, go straight to the trustees. Disputed material may be taken down while they take advice.
