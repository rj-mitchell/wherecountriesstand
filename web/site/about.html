About Where Countries Stand

Where Countries Stand prepares short, balanced briefings on what countries have said at the United Nations, and how they have voted.

AI briefings

Briefings are written by AI. Each one is labelled as written by AI, and each is checked against the records it cites before it is shown.

What the model is given

The AI that writes a briefing is given only the UN records found for your question, and may use nothing else, even for well-known facts, dates or names. Those records are statements made in the Security Council and in the General Assembly’s General Debate, and the General Assembly’s recorded votes (The record).

The newest General Debate speeches come from the UN’s website first and are labelled as not yet the official record. The General Debate’s verbatim records are published about a year after each debate. Until then, a speech comes from the UN’s own General Debate website: the text the delegation issued, as prepared, or an AI transcript of the UN’s English interpretation. The label sits beside the source, and the record replaces the text when it is published.

Evidence is drawn from across groups of countries: by region as a rule, by bloc where a question compares blocs, or by place at the Security Council (permanent, elected and invited members). The best statement is taken from each group in turn, with no more than two from any one country unless the question names the countries it is about. Briefings report in neutral words (Impartiality).

The UN’s records are read in twice a day: new Security Council meetings, General Assembly votes and General Debate records. The home page shows the latest date held for each, and the status page shows when the record was last read.

How it is checked

Each point links to its source, and each source to where it was published. Once a briefing is written, every citation is checked against the source text, quotations are checked word for word, and sentences that no source supports are flagged (Method gives the detail). The checks catch many errors but not all.

If the record holds nothing relevant, the site says “No records found”. When the search finds nothing at all, no briefing is written. When it finds only passing mentions, the AI is told to say “No records found” and what the records do cover.

The site holds Security Council verbatim records from 1992, and the General Assembly’s General Debate and recorded votes from 1946, all searchable and linked. Security Council statements, and General Debate statements taken from the UN’s verbatim records, carry their document symbols (S/PV, A/[session]/PV); votes carry their resolution numbers. Unless a question gives dates, a briefing draws on the last ten years.

The method and the code are published, every briefing is labelled as written by AI, and the site is free and doesn’t track you. The method is on this page, the code is linked under Licences, and the privacy notice sets out the little that is stored.

The record

The Security Council’s debates are held from 1992, from the UN Security Council Debates corpus (Schoenfeld and others, CC0) to the end of 2023, and from the UN’s verbatim records (S/PV) since, checked for new meetings twice a day.

The General Assembly’s General Debate is held in full: every country’s annual statement, from the UN General Debate Corpus (Jankin, Baturo and Dasandi, CC0) and, for recent sessions, the General Assembly’s verbatim records (A/[session]/PV). Until a session’s record is published, about a year after the debate, statements come from the UN’s General Debate website, and are marked as not yet the official record. Rights of reply are included as records of their own.

Every recorded vote in the General Assembly since 1946 comes from the UN Digital Library.

Method

The question is read for its subject, dates, countries and groups, shown as filters above each briefing. The record is searched by keyword and by meaning; statements by heads of State or Government and ministers carry a little more weight, and recent statements more than old ones. Countries are grouped by their memberships on the date. An AI model then writes the briefing from those sources only, and every sentence is checked against them: unsourced connecting sentences are shown in grey, and so are sentences whose citations all failed the check (the citations are removed), quotations not found word for word are marked, and judgemental words in the model’s own voice are underlined.

Impartiality

Briefings report the positions countries took and add no view of their own. The site’s own voice does not use labels such as “regime” or “rogue state”. Statements made on behalf of a group are attributed to the group. Countries are named as the UN names them.
