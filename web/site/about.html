About Where Countries Stand

Where Countries Stand prepares short, balanced briefings on what countries have said at the United Nations, and how they have voted.

Why use this rather than a chatbot?

Only the record. The AI that writes a briefing is given only the UN records found for your question, and may use nothing else: not even well-known facts, dates or names. Those records are statements made in the Security Council and in the General Assembly’s General Debate, and the General Assembly’s recorded votes. The newest General Debate statements come from the UN’s website until the verbatim record is published, and are marked as not yet the official record (The record).

Every point linked and checked. Each point links to its source, and each source to where it was published. Once a briefing is written, every citation is checked against the source text, and every quotation word for word. Any sentence that makes a point no source supports is flagged (How a briefing is made says how).

Up to date. The UN’s records are read in twice a day: new Security Council meetings, General Assembly votes and General Debate records. The home page shows the latest date held for each, and the Status page when the record was last read.

It says when there is nothing. If the record holds nothing relevant, you see “No records found”, not a guess. When the search finds nothing at all, no briefing is written. When it finds only passing mentions, the AI is told to say “No records found” and what the records do cover.

Balanced by design. Evidence is chosen across groups of countries, not only by relevance: by region as a rule, by bloc where a question compares blocs, or by place at the Security Council (permanent, elected and invited members). The best statement is taken from each group in turn, with no more than two from any one country unless the question names the countries it is about. Briefings report in neutral words (Impartiality).

Records searched in full. Security Council debates from 1992, every country’s statement in the General Debate from 1946 and the General Assembly’s recorded votes from 1946, all searchable and linked. Security Council statements, and General Debate statements taken from the UN’s verbatim records, carry their document symbols (S/PV, A/…/PV). Unless a question gives dates, a briefing draws on the last ten years.

Open. The method and the code are published, every briefing is labelled as written by AI, the site is free, and it doesn’t track you. The method is on this page, the code is linked under Licences, and the privacy notice sets out the little that is stored.

Briefings here are written by AI too. The difference is what the AI is given and how it is checked. How it works

The record

The Security Council. Its debates from 1992, from the UN Security Council Debates corpus (Schoenfeld and others, CC0) to the end of 2023, and from the UN’s verbatim records (S/PV) since, checked for new meetings twice a day.

The General Assembly’s General Debate. Every country’s annual statement, from the UN General Debate Corpus (Jankin, Baturo and Dasandi, CC0) and, for recent sessions, the General Assembly’s verbatim records (A/…/PV). Until a session’s record is published, about a year after the debate, statements come from the UN’s General Debate website, and are marked as not yet the official record. Rights of reply are included as records of their own.

General Assembly votes. Every recorded vote since 1946, from the UN Digital Library.

How a briefing is made

The question is read for its subject, dates, countries and groups, shown as filters above each briefing. The record is searched by keyword and by meaning; statements by heads of State or Government and ministers carry a little more weight, and recent statements more than old ones. Countries are grouped by their memberships on the date. An AI model then writes the briefing from those sources only, and every sentence is checked against them: unsourced connecting sentences are shown in grey, quotations not found word for word are marked, and judgemental words in the model’s own voice are underlined.

Impartiality

Briefings describe positions; they do not take them. The site’s own voice does not use labels such as “regime” or “rogue state”. Statements made on behalf of a group are attributed to the group. Countries are named as the UN names them.
